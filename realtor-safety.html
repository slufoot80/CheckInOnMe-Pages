The gap this fills

Most agents already have a safety habit: text a colleague the address before a showing, then text again when you're out. It works right up until the moment it matters — because the second text is the one that never gets sent, and nobody notices for hours.

That's the whole problem. The manual system depends on somebody else remembering to worry about you. A missed check-in should raise an alarm by itself.

SafePulse is the automated version of the habit you already have. You set the timer on the way in. If you come out fine, you tap once and nothing happens. If you don't, the alarm raises itself.

How it works

Three steps, and only the first one happens while you're busy.

1

Set a timer

Before you walk in, pick a window — anywhere from 30 minutes to 48 hours. A typical showing is 30 or 45 minutes.

2

Check in when you're out

One tap and the timer clears. No message to compose, nobody to notify, nothing else to remember.

3

Or don't — and they're told

If the timer expires, your trusted contacts get a text and an email with your last known location. They don't need the app.

Why it's built differently

Three design choices that matter for the way agents actually work.

⏱

Short windows, not daily check-ins

Most check-in apps are built around a once-a-day "are you okay" for someone living alone. That's the wrong shape for a showing. SafePulse starts at 30 minutes because the risk is the appointment, not the day.

📍

Location is inside the alert

The people you chose don't get a vague "she hasn't checked in." They get your last known location in the message itself, so the first thing they do is drive there, not start calling around.

📴

It fires with the phone off

The countdown is mirrored on a server. If your phone is switched off, out of battery, out of signal, or taken from you, the server sends the alert anyway. Nothing on your phone has to still be working.

What it is not

Worth stating plainly, because safety tools get oversold.

  • Not an emergency service. SafePulse does not call 911, police, fire, or EMS, and there is no monitoring center behind it. It notifies the contacts you chose. If you are in danger and able to call, call 911.
  • Not a panic button. A panic button needs you to be able to press it. This is the opposite design — it goes off when you can't act. Many agents carry both, and that's reasonable.
  • Not location tracking. Nobody can look up where you are while a check-in is running normally. Location is read for the alert and shared only if you miss the check-in. Your broker does not get a map of your day.
  • Not a guarantee. It depends on cell and internet service reaching your contacts. It reduces the hours between "something went wrong" and "somebody knows." It does not prevent anything.
  • Not a substitute for the basics. Meeting new clients at the office first, verifying identity, telling someone where you're going — keep doing all of it. This is a backstop for the times you didn't.

Questions agents ask

Does it track my location all day?

No. Location is read only while a check-in is running, and shared only if you miss one. Nobody — including your broker — can look up where you are while everything is fine.

What if my phone is taken or dies?

The alert still goes out. The countdown is mirrored on a server, so if your phone never checks in, the server sends the alert on its own.

Who gets the alert?

Contacts you choose — a spouse, your broker, an office manager, another agent. They get a text and an email with your last known location. They don't need the app installed.

What if I just forget to check in?

Your contacts get an alert and you'll have an awkward phone call. That's the correct failure mode for a safety tool — it should err toward telling someone.

Can my brokerage see my check-ins?

Only if you add them as a contact. There's no broker dashboard, no admin console, and no way for an office to monitor agents through this app.

Is there a subscription?

No. It's $4.99 once on Google Play. No subscription, no in-app purchases, no ads, and no selling of personal data.
