The gap this fills

Living alone is not the problem. Most people who live alone are fine, like it, and have no intention of moving in with anyone. The problem is narrower than that: there is no longer anyone in the building who would notice.

You already know the stories — the ones that end with found days later. What makes them land isn't the emergency. It's the gap between the emergency and anybody finding out. A fall on a Friday night, a stroke, a bad reaction to a new medication. In a house with someone else in it, that's an ambulance in ten minutes. Alone, the same event can go unnoticed until work calls on Monday.

The usual patch is a person: a daughter who calls every evening, a friend who texts in the morning. That works until the night she's travelling, or you don't pick up and she decides not to worry, or she does worry and spends two hours calling around before she thinks to drive over. It puts the job of noticing on someone who has their own life, and it is silent in exactly the way that matters — nothing happens when nothing happens.

SafePulse inverts that. Silence is the trigger. You tap once to say you're okay; if the tap doesn't come, the alarm raises itself, and it says where you are — not just that you've gone quiet.

Why it's built differently

There are simpler check-in apps and some are free. These are the things worth paying for — or not, depending on your situation.

📍

The alert says where you are

Most check-in apps tell your contact you've gone quiet. That's worrying, not actionable. SafePulse puts your last known location in the message, so the first thing your daughter does is drive there — not start phoning hospitals.

📴

It fires with the phone off

Your deadline is mirrored on a server. If your phone is switched off, out of battery, out of signal, or across the room where you can't reach it, the alert goes out anyway. Nothing on your phone has to still be working.

💬

Text, not just email

An email can sit unread all night. Contacts get a text message and an email, and up to 10 people can be on the list — so it isn't resting on one person happening to check their inbox.

⏱

Hours, not days

Apps built around a once-a-day tap generally wait for several missed days before telling anyone. If you fell on Friday, that's the wrong arithmetic. Here the window is yours to set, and a missed check-in is acted on the same day.

🚨

SOS when you're awake for it

Waiting for a timer isn't always right. An SOS button alerts everyone immediately, can auto-call your first contact, and can sound a siren on the phone to attract anyone nearby.

🔋

A dead battery says so

If your phone is about to die, your contacts are told that's what happened — so a flat battery isn't mistaken for a flat you, and nobody drives over at midnight for a charger.

What it is not

Worth stating plainly, because this is a corner of the app store where things get oversold to worried people.

  • Not a fall detector. There is no sensor watching for impact. SafePulse reacts to silence, not to a fall, so nothing happens until your check-in is due. If you want something that responds within seconds of hitting the floor, you want a fall-detection pendant or watch — a different product at a different price.
  • Not a medical alert service. There is no monitoring center and no human dispatcher. Nobody will call to see if you're all right or request a welfare check on your behalf. If you have nobody to list as a contact, a monitored service is genuinely the better answer — that's the part a one-time app purchase cannot replace.
  • Not an emergency service. SafePulse does not call 911, police, fire, or EMS. It notifies the people you chose. If you're in trouble and able to call, call 911.
  • Not location tracking. Nobody can look up where you are while your check-ins are running normally — not your children, not me. Location is read for the alert and shared only if you miss a check-in.
  • Not free, and free options exist. Snug Safety gives away a daily check-in with alerts to your contacts. If you're setting this up for a parent

Most people who buy a check-in app are not the person who will be tapping the button.

It goes on their phone; you're the contact. They tap once a day. You do nothing at all unless they stop — then you get a text and an email with their last known location. No daily report, no “all clear” notification to skim past and stop reading after a fortnight.

There's no dashboard, and that's deliberate. You cannot open the app and see where your mother is. A tool that lets adult children watch their parents gets uninstalled, usually in the first week, and fairly. This one only speaks up when something is wrong, which is the version a parent will actually leave running.

They have to agree to it. Contacts confirm by reply before they are stored or texted, so nobody is added to anything behind their back — including you.

Questions people ask

How often do I have to check in?

You decide — anywhere from every 30 minutes to every 48 hours. Once a day is what most people use at home. You can change it whenever you like, or turn it off while you've got company.

What if I just forget?

You get a reminder first, then a loud full-screen warning while there's still time to tap. If you sleep through all of it, your contacts get an alert and you'll have a slightly embarrassing phone call. That's the correct failure mode — a safety tool should err toward telling someone.

Does it detect falls?

No. There is no fall sensor. It works on missed check-ins, which covers a fall, a medical episode, or anything else that stops you reaching your phone — but it reacts to silence rather than to impact, so nothing happens until your check-in is due. Nothing to wear, and no button you have to be conscious enough to press.

What if my phone is off or the battery dies?

The alert still goes out. Your deadline is mirrored on a server, so if your phone never checks in, the server sends the alert on its own. Your contacts are also warned separately if the phone is simply running out of battery.

Who gets the alert, and do they need the app?

People you choose — up to 10 of them. They get a text message and an email with your last known location, and they don't need the app, an account, or a smartphone. They confirm by reply before they're added, so nobody is signed up without knowing.

Can my family see where I am?

Only if you miss a check-in. There's no map, no dashboard and no way for anyone — including me — to look up where you are while everything is fine. Location is read for the alert and shared only when one is sent.

Can I set it up for my mother or father?

Yes, and plenty of people do. The app goes on their phone and you're the contact. Check which phone they have first: on Android it's a one-time purchase you can buy for them, and on iPhone the subscription has to be set up on their own Apple ID.

Aren't there free apps that do this?

Yes — Snug Safety's free tier does a daily check-in with alerts to your contacts, and Demumu is about a dollar. If that covers your situation, use it. Is this a medical alert or monitoring service?

No. There's no monitoring center and no dispatcher who will call you or send someone to check. It tells the people you chose. If you don't have anyone to list, a monitored service is the honest recommendation — it costs a great deal more per month, and what you're paying for is a human being.
