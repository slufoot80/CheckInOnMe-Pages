The gap this fills

The schedule says you have a 2:00 in a house you've never been to. It does not say when you came out. If the 3:00 gets missed, somebody might call. If it was the last visit of the day, nobody is expecting you anywhere at all.

Most home care safety advice ends at “tell someone where you're going.” You already do that — it's on the schedule. The part that's missing is the other half: somebody noticing you didn't come back. That half depends on a coworker remembering to worry about you, at the end of their own ten-visit day.

SafePulse closes that half automatically. You set the timer on the doorstep. If you come out fine, you tap once and nothing happens. If you don't, the alarm raises itself.

How it works

Three steps, and only the first one happens while you're busy.

1

Start a timer at the door

Pick a window — anywhere from 30 minutes to 48 hours. Give yourself the length you actually expect, plus a margin.

2

Check in when you're back in the car

One tap and the timer clears. No message to compose, nobody to notify, nothing else to remember at the end of a long day.

3

Or don't — and they're told

If the timer expires, your trusted contacts get a text and an email with your last known location. They don't need the app.

Why it's built differently

Three design choices that matter for the way home visits actually go.

⏱

Visit-length windows, not daily check-ins

Most check-in apps are built around a once-a-day “are you okay” for someone living alone. That's the wrong shape for a caseload. SafePulse starts at 30 minutes because the risk is the visit, not the day — and you may run six of them.

📍

Location is inside the alert

The people you chose don't get a vague “she hasn't checked in.” They get your last known location in the message itself. That matters when your route covers four towns and nobody can guess which stop you're on.

📴

It fires with the phone off

The countdown is mirrored on a server. If your phone is switched off, out of battery, out of signal, or taken from you, the server sends the alert anyway. A phone that's flat by the eighth visit is a normal Tuesday — the alert shouldn't die with it.

What it is not

Worth stating plainly, because safety tools get oversold — and you are trained to read the fine print.

  • Not an emergency service. SafePulse does not call 911, police, fire, or EMS, and there is no monitoring center behind it. It notifies the contacts you chose. If you are in danger and able to call, call 911.
  • Not a panic button. A panic button needs you to be able to press it. This is the opposite design — it goes off when you can't act. Carrying both is reasonable.
  • Not a clinical system, and not HIPAA-covered. There is no business associate agreement, and the app holds nothing about your patients. It needs to stay that way — see the note below.
  • Not location tracking. Nobody can look up where you are while a check-in is running normally. Location is read for the alert and shared only if you miss the check-in. Your agency does not get a map of your day.
  • Not a guarantee. It depends on cellular and internet service reaching your contacts. It shortens the gap between “something went wrong” and “somebody knows.” It does not prevent anything.
  • Not a substitute for the basics. Pre-visit risk screening, your agency's own protocol, and your right to leave a home that feels wrong — keep all of it. This is a backstop, not a replacement.

Please don't put patient information in it. SafePulse is a personal safety tool for you, not a clinical record. Don't type patient names, addresses, diagnoses, or any protected health information into check-in labels or notes. If you label a check-in at all, use the street you're on. When an alert fires, the alert text, your chosen contacts' phone numbers and email addresses, and your last known location are sent to our messaging gateway so it can deliver the alert when your phone can't — which is exactly why nothing about a patient belongs in there. Full detail is in the privacy policy.

Questions nurses ask

Is this a HIPAA tool? Can I put patient information in it?

No, and please don't. It's a personal safety tool for you, not a clinical system, and there is no business associate agreement. Keep patient names, addresses and diagnoses out of it. If you label a check-in, use the street you're on — or nothing at all.

Does my agency see where I am?

Only if you choose to add someone from the agency as a contact, and even then only when you miss a check-in. There's no supervisor dashboard and no admin console. You pick the contacts, and you can remove them whenever you want.

What if my phone is taken, dies, or has no signal in the house?

The alert still goes out. The countdown is mirrored on a server, so if your phone never checks in, the server sends the alert on its own. That matters more in home care than almost anywhere else — a phone that's flat by the eighth visit is a normal Tuesday, not a freak event.

Visits run long constantly. Won't it fire all the time?

Set the window to what you actually expect plus a margin — it goes up to 48 hours, so there's no reason to cut it fine. If a visit is running past your window, check in and start a fresh timer. And if one does fire because you got busy, your contacts call you and you have an awkward thirty seconds. That's the correct failure mode for a safety tool.

Who gets the alert?

Contacts you choose — a partner, a scheduler, your clinical manager, another nurse on the team. They get a text and an email with your last known location. They don't need the app installed.

I carry controlled substances. Does that change anything?

Not in how the app works, but it's often the reason people go looking for something like this. The app has no idea what's in your bag and doesn't want to know. It only answers one question: did you come back out when you said you would.

My agency already gave us a safety protocol. Why this too?

Because most protocols cover what to do before and during a visit, and depend on a person to notice afterward. This is the afterward part, automated. It's a backstop for the day the noticing doesn't happen.
